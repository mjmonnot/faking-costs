Key references

A short list, not a dump. Each entry says in a sentence why a practitioner might open it. Full citations and the complete reference list are in the manuscript.

  • Stanek, K. C., & Ones, D. S. (2023). Personality Traits and Cognitive Abilities Relations Database. doi:10.13020/7YS8-ET75 The public database Study 1 re-analyzes: thousands of personality–ability correlations with sample-type codes. Open license (CC BY-NC-ND); you can check any number on this site against it.
  • Sackett, P. R., Zhang, C., Berry, C. M., & Lievens, F. (2022). Revisiting meta-analytic estimates of validity in personnel selection. Journal of Applied Psychology. The current standard for operational validities — the .31 (ability) and .19 (Conscientiousness) the calculator uses — and the paper that reset expectations about range-restriction corrections.
  • Birkeland, S. A., Manson, T. M., Kisamore, J. L., Brannick, M. T., & Smith, M. A. (2006). A meta-analytic investigation of job applicant faking on personality measures. International Journal of Selection and Assessment. The meta-analytic applicant–incumbent mean differences (Conscientiousness d = .45) every simulation here is required to reproduce.
  • Griffith, R. L., Chmielowski, T., & Yoshita, Y. (2007). Do applicants fake? Personnel Review. The classic within-person demonstration: the same people tested as applicants and again honestly. The source of the 30–50% prevalence range, with Donovan, Dwight, & Schneider (2014) replicating.
  • Griffith, R. L., & Converse, P. D. (2011). The rules of evidence and the prevalence of applicant faking. In M. Ziegler, C. MacCann, & R. D. Roberts (Eds.), New perspectives on faking in personality assessment. The synthesis that concludes roughly 30% ± 10% of applicants fake — the basis for this site’s headline and the calculator’s default.
  • Holtrop, D., Oostrom, J. K., Dunlop, P. D., & Runneboom, C. (2021). Predictors of faking behavior on personality inventories in selection. Applied Psychology: An International Review. The study of genuine applicants that measured who fakes: cognitive ability correlated −.19 with score elevation — the compensatory pattern at the center of this site.
  • Weißenbacher, D., & Krammer, G. (2025). Applicant faking in teacher-education admission. Journal of Research in Personality. A within-subjects counterpoint from a different selection setting (teacher-education admission), where ability weakly positively predicted inflation — a reminder the direction can depend on context, which is why the manuscript scopes its claims to job-applicant settings.
  • Ones, D. S., Viswesvaran, C., & Reiss, A. D. (1996). Role of social desirability in personality testing for personnel selection. Journal of Applied Psychology. The meta-analytic correlations between each Big Five trait and social desirability scales — the basis for the manuscript’s desirability-proportional inflation model, and the reason "everyone exaggerates equally" is a testable (and failing) account.
  • Howard, M. C., & Van Zandt, E. C. (2020). A meta-analysis of the honesty-humility–workplace outcome relationship. Journal of Research in Personality. One of the two integrity anchors behind the 0.2–0.8 SD integrity-gap range for faked-in hires (the other: overt integrity-test correlations).
  • Schmidt, F. L., & Hunter, J. E. (1983). Individual differences in productivity. Journal of Applied Psychology. The utility convention (performance SD in dollars ≈ 40% of salary) behind every dollar figure on this site.
  • Dahlke, J. A., & Sackett, P. R. (2017). The relationship between cognitive-ability saturation and subgroup mean differences. Journal of Applied Psychology; and Roth, P. L., et al. (2011) on applicant-pool ability differences. The subgroup-difference inputs behind the adverse-impact ratios — the statistic the simulations show staying quiet while hires change.
  • NYC Local Law 144 (2021). The emerging audit regime for automated hiring tools. Its required statistic — selection-rate impact ratios — is the one these simulations show is blind to faking’s composition costs.

Monnot, M. J. (2026). Compensatory faking and the relationship between cognitive ability and personality in personnel selection: Meta-analytic evidence and trait-driven simulations [Manuscript in preparation]. Preprint link to follow.
